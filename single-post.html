Emergency care

When should you go to Emergency instead of waiting for OPD?

Some symptoms can safely wait for a scheduled OPD visit. Others need to be seen straight away. Here is a plain-language guide to help you tell the difference.

By RK Hospital Team 24×7 emergency in Barrackpore

Deciding whether to head to the emergency department or wait for the outpatient department (OPD) can be stressful — especially at night or when a family member is unwell. The simple rule is this: the emergency department is for problems that are sudden, severe, or could get worse quickly, while OPD is for planned check-ups, ongoing conditions and non-urgent concerns.

This article is general information to help you decide, not a diagnosis. If you are ever unsure and the situation feels serious, treat it as an emergency and get medical help.

What the emergency department is for

An emergency department is set up to assess and stabilise people quickly, at any hour. Patients are seen in order of how urgent their condition is (triage), not simply in order of arrival. Go to Emergency — or call for help — if you notice any of the following:

  • Chest pain, pressure or tightness, especially with sweating, nausea or breathlessness.
  • Sudden difficulty breathing or shortness of breath at rest.
  • Sudden weakness or numbness on one side, drooping face, or slurred speech (possible stroke).
  • Heavy bleeding that will not stop, or a serious injury after a fall or road accident.
  • A seizure, fainting, or sudden confusion.
  • A very high fever in a young infant, or a child who is unusually drowsy or hard to wake.
  • Severe, sudden abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, or signs of dehydration.

When OPD is the right choice

Many health concerns are important but not urgent, and are better handled in OPD, where your doctor has time to examine you, order tests and plan follow-up. OPD usually suits:

  • Routine check-ups and follow-up visits for a known condition.
  • Long-standing symptoms such as mild aches, a cough that is improving, or a skin problem.
  • Reviewing reports, adjusting regular medicines, or getting a referral.
  • Non-urgent second opinions and health advice.

If you are not sure

When symptoms sit somewhere in between, a few questions can help:

  • Did this come on suddenly, or has it built up slowly over days?
  • Is it getting worse quickly, or staying much the same?
  • Is the person struggling to breathe, stay awake, or stay steady?

If the answer points to sudden, worsening, or severe, it is safer to be seen in Emergency. You will never be turned away for asking. When in doubt, you can call the hospital and describe what is happening before you travel.

What to bring with you

  • A list of current medicines and any known allergies.
  • Previous reports or discharge papers, if you have them to hand.
  • Identity and any insurance, TPA or health-scheme details for smoother admission.
